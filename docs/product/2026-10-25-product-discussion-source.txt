LuLu is a private documentation and support environment disguised within a genuinely useful everyday application. Its purpose is to let someone capture an experience, preserve it, understand their options and eventually seek assistance, without forcing them to disclose their identity or take action before they are ready.

The key distinction is continuity. A person shouldn't have to move between a notes application, evidence recorder, safety app, messaging service and support directory to deal with different aspects of the same experience.

The design separates three kinds of data:

Original private record. The authoritative text, audio, image or document stays in the encrypted local vault. It retains its authorship and provenance.

Protected synchronisation copy. With the relevant feature enabled, encrypted records can be backed up or made available to an authorised companion. This is distinct from sending readable content to an AI provider.

Disclosure copy. A separate, redacted representation created for a defined purpose, such as requesting professional advice or running an external classification.

The user continues to own and control the original record. No model or recipient should silently rewrite it.

A crucial detail: the Decisions API is a structured AI decision service, but structured outputs alone don't guarantee that it cannot make errors. Deterministic validation, local privacy checks and explicit disclosure policies still matter.

3. The main application flows
A. Onboarding and ordinary usage

Launch directly into a useful notes/to-do experience, with normal writing, task creation and organisation.
Introduce the private capability discreetly, including what remains on-device and what optional sharing or processing involves.
Register a passkey and establish device-local protection for the private vault.
Provide optional recovery and trusted-companion setup once the user can complete it safely.
Populate the ordinary surface with clearly labelled sample or generated content for the owner, kept completely separate from authentic records.
B. Entering private mode

A user should be able to enter from more than one context: a deliberate gesture in the ordinary editor, a protected note, a designated quick-action control or a configured shortcut. Each route opens the same authenticated privacy boundary.

Successful passkey verification unlocks the private session and permits access to selected local records. A quick-capture shortcut can first capture into an encrypted, pending local record, provided the device can protect it securely, with authentication required to review or organise it afterwards.

C. Private writing and capture

Private writing uses an editor that supports paragraphs and embedded evidence. Individual passages can be concealed, revealed or included in a separately redacted disclosure copy.

Images, voice recordings and attachments belong to records but retain their original form. A transcript or annotation is derived material, never a replacement for the original audio.

A capture can become a journal entry, incident record or unclassified private note. The user doesn't need to determine its legal or psychological meaning when recording it.

D. Exiting private mode

A quick exit removes sensitive content from the screen, navigates to an ordinary safe surface, locks the private session and prevents sensitive information from appearing in app previews.

Normal exit may save the current private work and return to the ordinary editor. Automatic concealment can also occur on inactivity or when the application moves into the background.

The recording behaviour of quick exit must be predictable: a user should know whether capture stops and whether the last recording was saved. Quick exit should not silently continue recording.

E. Understanding and receiving help

A user selects a record or set of records to understand. LuLu evaluates identifying-information risks and potentially recurring themes, then shows explanatory information and available support categories without issuing a diagnosis.

From there, the user may keep the information private, save the interpretation, create a disclosure copy, contact a trusted companion or eventually speak to a verified professional.

4. Trusted companions, evidence and Trails
Your latest addition makes the companion substantially more useful than an emergency contact.

A trusted companion is an authorised participant in a private support relationship, with narrowly defined access.

They might be permitted to receive a selected voice note, view a particular record, contribute a statement, retain an encrypted backup, or follow a temporary Trail when someone feels unsafe.

Their contributions must be separate from the owner's originals.

For example:

Illustrative evidence history

Original record

Owner · 14:32 · Audio recording created

Supplementary statement

Trusted companion · 15:06 · Added contextual information

Disclosure version

Redacted copy prepared for a particular recipient

The evidence model therefore needs immutable originals, append-only changes or events, version history, actor attribution and content hashes. Hashes can indicate whether bytes have changed; they do not by themselves establish that a reported incident occurred or prove an exact real-world capture time.

Trails introduces another record type: a time-bounded series of location observations captured with the owner's permission. It could be viewed live by an authorised companion or retained as part of the owner's record.

Location history is exceptionally sensitive. LuLu needs clear control over when a Trail begins, who can view it, how long sharing lasts, and whether historical points are retained. An ordinary journal backup should not automatically grant access to Trails.

For recovery, trusted storage could use encrypted copies held by a companion without necessarily giving that person the ability to read every private record. That separates backup authority from viewing authority.

6. Where the Decisions API fits
The decision layer has two responsibilities, which are related but distinct.

The first is privacy assessment: identify direct PII, indirect identifying context and potentially sensitive combinations of information. The second is content interpretation: identify themes that might help someone understand the experiences they've described.

For example, a person's journal might repeatedly discuss being prevented from accessing money. LuLu could offer information about financial control as a recognised concern, show the relevant descriptions that prompted this suggestion, and present support resources or the option of speaking to a professional.

It does not need to conclude that the person is experiencing a particular legally or clinically defined form of abuse.

The next architectural step is to define typed decision contracts, including their inputs, allowed outputs and which ones are permitted to receive locally sanitised information.
