Lean Business Canvas

Current draft · saved in Sonke

Full nine-section canvas, organised for reading. Costs and pricing are planning assumptions.

01

Problem Statement

Gender-based violence, harassment and abuse remain significant challenges in South Africa. The HSRC's First South African National Gender-Based Violence Study, 2022, published in 2024, found that 35.5% of women aged 18 and older had experienced physical and/or sexual violence in their lifetime.

Beyond the violence itself, people face barriers to documenting incidents discreetly, preserving evidence, accessing appropriate support and deciding whether to disclose or report their experiences. Records may be fragmented across messages, photographs and personal accounts, while seeking assistance can mean revealing sensitive information before someone is ready. LuLu addresses the need for accessible, survivor-directed documentation and support that preserves the individual's control over their information.

02

Solution & Features

LuLu is a discreet, mobile-first personal safety and documentation platform built around a useful everyday journal and storybook experience.

The planned protected experience combines concealed, passkey-mediated access with incident documentation, supporting photographs, messages, audio and documents, and an organised chronology. Contextual transformation changes identifying details and narrative perspective into coherent alternative accounts while preserving protected access to the author's original meaning. Ordinary journalling remains available without activating privacy processing.

The product direction includes evidence-integrity records, selective disclosure, discreet exit, cross-device continuity and recovery, and relevant support and referral pathways. Privacy-preserving insights into recurring patterns remain exploratory. Users choose whether, when and how to seek assistance or share information.

03

Unique Value Proposition

Your experience. Your evidence. Your decision.

LuLu is designed to help people document sensitive experiences discreetly, preserve important information and explore support options without pressure to disclose or formally report.

By bringing an everyday journal together with protected access, contextual narrative concealment, evidence organisation and selective sharing, LuLu aims to preserve continuity and control from the first record to whatever step the individual chooses next.

04

Unfair Advantage

LuLu's intended competitive advantage is the integration of discreet access, contextual narrative transformation, evidence continuity and user-controlled disclosure within an authentic everyday writing experience.

This combines capabilities that people would otherwise have to manage across separate journalling, storage and support tools. The team's privacy-oriented development experience provides a foundation for building and refining that integrated experience.

Longer-term defensibility will depend on the quality and consistency of the privacy capability, user trust and validated support relationships. These are advantages to develop and demonstrate, rather than claims of exclusive technology or existing partnerships.

05

Customer Segments

Primary users: People in South Africa who need to document experiences involving gender-based violence, domestic abuse, harassment, intimidation, coercion or other personal safety concerns, regardless of gender. This includes men who may face stigma when seeking support or reporting abuse.

Early adoption communities: University students, young adults and digitally connected communities reached through accessible information and trusted referrals.

Secondary users: People seeking discreet personal documentation, sensitive journalling and greater control over personal information.

Potential institutional customers and partners: NGOs, victim-support organisations, educational institutions and community programmes that could support or sponsor access. These are prospective relationships, not established partnerships.

06

Key Metrics

Primary measure: Whether LuLu helps an individual document their experience, access appropriate support and make progress towards an outcome they choose. Meaningful help for a small number of people matters more than a large registration count.

Impact indicators: Successful documentation and preservation of supporting material; continuity and retrieval of records when needed; voluntary feedback on confidence, comfort and control; successful connections to appropriate assistance; and user-reported progress towards personal goals. Goals may include privately preserving an account, seeking counselling or protection, or choosing to report.

During a controlled pilot, record the number and proportion of consenting participants who achieve their chosen documentation or support goals, alongside usability failures and privacy incidents. Use optional, minimal feedback without collecting incident narratives for analytics. Establish baselines before setting numerical targets; do not equate reporting or convictions with success for every user.

07

Distribution Channels

Initial delivery: A mobile-first web application accessible through a browser, with native iOS and Android applications planned as the product develops.

Early awareness and adoption: South African university communities, student organisations, community networks, relevant digital communities and referrals from trusted individuals.

Partnership channels to explore: GBV and victim-support organisations, community NGOs, educational institutions, counselling networks and social-impact programmes. These relationships could support trusted introductions and sponsored access.

Hackathons, product demonstrations and privacy-awareness content can create initial visibility. Communications should respect discretion and avoid revealing someone's use of sensitive features. Channels and partnerships will be tested with potential users rather than assumed to be established.

08

Cost Structure

Indicative incremental monthly budget for development and a small controlled pilot:

Hosting, authentication, database and server functions: R0–R300.
AI processing: R0–R500, initially supported by available API credits.
Evidence storage and data transfer: R0–R300.
Monitoring, testing and supporting services: R0–R200.
Additional domain and deployment expenses: R0–R100 where existing resources can be reused.

Total planning range: R0–R1,400 per month. These are estimates, not supplier quotes or measured operating costs. The lower end assumes existing resources, available allowances and credits; credits fund consumption rather than remove its underlying cost.

The estimate excludes development labour, hardware, legal/privacy review, professional services and maintaining support partnerships. Costs will be measured against active usage, processing volume and attachment storage, with budgets and usage alerts before scaling.

09

Revenue Streams

Proposed sustainable-access model:

Free individual access to essential private documentation, protected journal entries, evidence organisation and support resources.

Optional premium subscription, indicatively R39–R59 per month, for expanded storage, enhanced narrative capabilities and additional organisation features. Pricing and willingness to pay require validation. For illustration only, 100 subscribers at R49 would generate R4,900 per month in gross subscription revenue, before costs, fees and taxes; this is not a forecast.

Institutional sponsorship and service agreements could fund access through NGOs, educational institutions and community organisations. Grants, foundations and corporate social-investment programmes are additional funding routes to explore.

Longer term, the privacy transformation capability may support business integrations or technology licensing. This is a separate future opportunity. The model does not rely on selling private narratives, evidence or identifying information.

SSDLC security plan

This is the architecture-led draft we prepared. You submitted the SSDLC after your own edits; this copy may differ from the final submitted wording.

01

Security Requirements (Risk Assessment)

LuLu is being designed as an everyday journal and storybook with a discreet protected experience for documenting abuse, harassment and other difficult experiences. The security goal is to help people preserve their records, maintain continuity and choose when and how to seek support or disclose information. This includes users of all genders facing shared-device access, stigma or coercive control.

Our risk assessment prioritises original narratives, identifying details, private associations, supporting evidence and access/recovery material. The principal risks are unintended discovery, unauthorised disclosure, record alteration or loss, and loss of access when a device becomes unavailable.

The proposed requirements are protected access, device-controlled private associations, encryption with explicit key custody, data minimisation, discreet session exit, evidence integrity and user-controlled sharing. Ordinary journaling should remain useful without activating sensitive processing. Support/referral pathways should let users seek help without automatically disclosing their records. These requirements will guide implementation and verification throughout development.

02

Threat Modelling & Design Review

Our design review will trace information across the ordinary journal, protected experience, device storage, synchronisation, AI processing, recovery and administration. Storage and AI services will be assessed separately, with an explicit account of what each can receive and access.

The intended privacy layer recognises people, places, times and contextual relationships and produces a coherent concealed narrative while preserving the author's ability to retrieve their original meaning. Private associations are intended to remain under device-side control. Concealment will complement authentication and cryptographic protection.

Threat scenarios will include access to an unlocked device, coerced access, exposed sessions or backups, malicious scripts, misconfigured permissions, revealing metadata and incorrect entity associations. Design responses include passkey-mediated protected access, session clearing on exit, minimal sensitive telemetry and separation of administrative privileges from private content. Original evidence and transformed representations will remain distinguishable. Recovery and authorised device access will be designed to support continuity without creating an unnoticed access route. Residual risks outside the app's control will be documented.

03

Development (Secure coding practices)

We will develop LuLu using modular components and explicit boundaries between the journal interface, privacy processing, protected access, persistence, evidence handling and external services. This makes security responsibilities reviewable and allows processing methods to evolve without changing the user's writing experience.

Our spec-driven workflow will connect product requirements to design decisions, implementation tasks and verification evidence. Security-sensitive changes will receive review before integration. Practices will include input and model-output validation, safe rendering of user content, least-privilege authorisation, protected service credentials, dependency review and separation of development, test and deployment environments.

Sensitive narratives, private associations, keys and credentials must stay out of repositories, diagnostic logs and analytics. Attachment handling will include file validation, access controls and careful treatment of metadata and previews. Original records will be protected from accidental overwrite by narrative transformations. External AI output will be treated as untrusted data and will not grant access or trigger disclosure. Exact cryptographic and recovery mechanisms will be recorded as architecture decisions and verified during implementation.

04

Security Testing

Our security test plan will follow the user's full journey: ordinary writing, protected access, contextual transformation, correction, saving, retrieval, exit, continuity and selective disclosure. Synthetic scenarios will cover both successful use and attempts to cross trust boundaries.

Testing will include unauthorised and cross-user access, session expiry, exit followed by back navigation or reload, and checks for sensitive content in storage, network traffic, logs, notifications and previews. Privacy-processing tests will examine missed identifiers, revealing context, incorrect associations, cross-entry consistency and correction without damage to the original record.

Evidence and continuity tests will cover original-versus-transformed records, attachment integrity, interrupted writes, device loss, authorised recovery and deletion behaviour. Integration checks will cover storage permissions, API authorisation, malicious inputs, secret exposure and dependencies. Results will identify the tested revision and environment, observed behaviour and unresolved findings. These are planned security checks; completion will be supported by recorded evidence rather than inferred from a working interface.

05

Assessment & Secure Integration

We will assess LuLu against its central outcomes: discreet documentation, preserved meaning and evidence, dependable continuity and user control over disclosure. Integration decisions will be reviewed against the risk assessment and trust boundaries, with security findings feeding back into design and implementation.

Before release, the team will review data flows, encryption and key custody, protected-session behaviour, recovery, retention/deletion, least-privilege permissions and dependency findings. Cloud storage and AI processing will each require a clear purpose and minimum necessary data. Device-local processing is the intended direction for privacy transformation; any hosted processing used for the hackathon will have its data exposure and limitations explicitly documented.

Sharing and referral flows will require the user's deliberate choice of recipient and material. Original evidence, derived narratives and exports will have distinguishable provenance. The review will record verified controls, remaining risks, accountable follow-up and release decisions. High-impact unresolved exposure will be addressed before handling real sensitive records. This assessment describes the intended security lifecycle; implementation and test evidence will establish which controls are ready for use.

Screen-by-screen record

Chapter 1 — Personalise (1–3)

01 · Meet LuLu

  • Purpose: Introduce the journal and its purpose.
  • Pictured headline/copy: “Meet LuLu.” / “An ordinary journal with an extraordinary purpose.”
  • Action and controls: Continue button with arrow; three short progress marks near the bottom. No Skip is pictured on screen 1.
  • Interaction/data: Continue to screen 2. No user data is requested.
  • Visual treatment: Full-bleed warm photograph of a journal, soft light and a plant; white LuLu wordmark and white editorial text over the image. This screen is more photographic than the later cards.

02 · What should we call you?

  • Purpose: Offer optional personalisation.
  • Pictured headline/copy: “What should we call you?” / “Choose a name or nickname to make this space feel like yours.” The reassurance reads “This name stays on your device. It’s private and never shared.”
  • Action and controls: Name field (placeholder “Your name”, example “e.g. Alex”); Skip; back arrow; Continue.
  • Interaction/data: Entering a name personalises screen 4. Skip should continue with neutral copy. The pictured on-device storage and privacy statement is a claim to verify against the eventual storage path; do not repeat it in product UI unless it is true for that data on each supported client.
  • Visual treatment: Warm ivory surface, black wordmark, large serif headline, bordered rounded input, soft reassurance panel and sage Continue button.

03 · What brings you here today?

  • Purpose: Let the user identify helpful starting areas.
  • Pictured headline/copy: “What brings you here today?” / “Choose what you’d like LuLu to help with first.” Options: “Write privately — A safe space for your thoughts”; “Capture thoughts — Reflect on your day and feelings”; “Understand my options — Gain clarity with thoughtful guidance”; “Find support — Feel less alone on your journey”; “Save important moments — Keep track of what matters to you”.
  • Action and controls: Five selectable cards, Skip, back arrow and Continue. The picture does not make the selection rule (single vs multi-select) explicit; source discussion calls this a multi-select experience.
  • Interaction/data: Selections may tailor later prompt/support examples. Treat choices as preference data, not consent to share, seek help, use interpretation, or enable private mode. Skip should proceed with generic onboarding.
  • Visual treatment: Five elevated ivory cards in a two-column arrangement with simple line icons, sage/sand icon grounds and a full-width final card.

Chapter 2 — First entry (4–6)

04 · Personal welcome

  • Purpose: Connect the selected intentions to a personal welcome.
  • Pictured headline/copy: “Thandi, you’re in the right place.” / “Whatever brings you here, there’s room for your story.” A privacy note reads “The name you choose stays on this device.”
  • Action and controls: Skip, back arrow and “Let’s begin”. Global progress is 4 / 14.
  • Interaction/data: Substitute the optional chosen name; if no name was provided, use a neutral greeting. Continue to screen 5. The personalisation promise inherits screen 2’s storage verification requirement.
  • Visual treatment: Warm journal photograph and large white editorial text; translucent lower note and sage action.

05 · Choose a starting point

  • Purpose: Offer a low-pressure entry into writing.
  • Pictured headline/copy: “Every story starts somewhere.” / “Choose a prompt to create your first entry.” Cards: “What feels important today?”, “Is there something you want to remember?”, “What has been on your mind lately?”, and “Start with a blank page.” Note: “Prompts can adapt to what brings you here as you continue.”
  • Action and controls: Four selectable prompt cards, Skip, back and Continue. Selecting a prompt supplies an optional writing prompt on screen 6; the pictured Continue is the main path.
  • Interaction/data: Selection should not force an answer. Adapt prompts only from screen 3 preferences and keep a neutral blank-page option. No OS permissions are needed here.
  • Visual treatment: Four softly elevated cards with concise copy/icons on warm ivory; light note panel and sage Continue.

06 · Your first entry

  • Purpose: Let the user experience ordinary writing and create a first entry.
  • Pictured headline/copy: “Your first entry.” / “Take a moment to write freely. There’s no right or wrong way.” The example prompt says “What feels important today?” The example body begins “The morning was quieter than usual, and I found myself noticing the small things — the light through the leaves, a slower pace, and a clearer sense of what I want to focus on.” Status says “Saved on this device”; the image also shows “128 words”.
  • Action and controls: Editor with visible cursor; image and microphone affordances; divider; bold, italic and link controls; word count; back; “Save entry”. The selected prompt is optional. The discussion says attachment actions should not request permission until used. After a successful save, show a subtle “Saved on this device” confirmation.
  • Interaction/data: Save must report success only after durable persistence. Explain where the draft resides and what happens if the user exits before screen 14. This is the critical unresolved pre-enrolment storage boundary: an entry can contain highly sensitive material, but the user has not yet enrolled or authenticated. Do not put it in ordinary sample data, a shared preview, analytics, remote storage or an unprotected browser store. Define encryption/key ownership, crash recovery, cancellation, deletion and the transition into the account before building this path.
  • Visual treatment: Warm paper editor card, restrained serif writing text and minimal toolbar. “Saved on this device” is shown as a state, not decoration.

Chapter 3 — Private layer (7–10)

07 · Some things are just yours

  • Purpose: Explain the distinction between everyday journal writing and the protected space.
  • Pictured headline/copy: “Some things are just yours.” / “LuLu gives you a private space for experiences you’d rather keep to yourself.”
  • Action and controls: “Discover private mode”, back arrow and Skip.
  • Interaction/data: Advance to the demonstration on screen 8. A normal-looking journal card transitions to a dark concealed card. The pictured example is illustrative only; it must not expose a real private entry or silently convert this onboarding entry into a protected record.
  • Visual treatment: Warm ordinary card above a charcoal/sage concealed card, with a transition arrow and lock cue.

08 · Your private space, one gesture away

  • Purpose: Demonstrate the intended private-entry affordance and verification step.
  • Pictured headline/copy: “Your private space, one gesture away.” / “Use your private shortcut to enter a protected space. LuLu verifies it’s you before revealing private content.” The sheet reads “Verify it’s you”, “Use Face ID to access your private space.”, “Continue with Face ID”, and “Use passkey instead”.
  • Action and controls: Illustrative trigger → system-style authentication sheet → protected-content transition; Skip and Continue are also pictured.
  • Interaction/data: The private-entry gesture is still undecided. A gesture, top plus, “Private” tab or lock drawing on this board must not reverse the established discreet-access direction or be treated as a granted permission. The source discussion explicitly says the passkey here is a demonstration; actual enrolment comes later at screen 14. Use synthetic sample text and a simulated state transition; do not invoke a real authenticator or expose real private data here.
  • Visual treatment: Dark charcoal screen behind a warm compact verification sheet, fingerprint illustration, muted blurred journal controls and contrasting white/sage action.

09 · Protect what matters

  • Purpose: Teach paragraph-level concealment while preserving the author’s source text.
  • Pictured headline/copy: “Protect what matters.” / “Choose what stays visible. Your original words remain preserved.” The example record is “A calmer morning”; the concealed block reads “This paragraph is private”.
  • Action and controls: Example entry with three paragraphs, overflow menu, image/bold/italic/link toolbar, back, Continue. Source discussion proposes select paragraph → conceal → reveal while authenticated.
  • Interaction/data: This is a demonstration on sample content only. If blur/masking is used, clearly distinguish temporary visual concealment from deleting/removing text; the image uses a blurred block and lock indicator. 10 · Leave in an instant

  • Purpose: Show the quick exit result and return to ordinary space.

  • Interaction/data: Simulate only. Do not lock/clear a real user session or alter real records from this training screen. When the actual app later implements quick exit, its established behavior is immediate lock and removal of callbacks/queued references, without silently flushing unsaved plaintext.
  • Visual treatment: Warm ordinary journal in front, darker concealed records behind, compact confirmation card and clear transition arrow.

Chapter 4 — Capture, understand, connect, secure (11–14)

11 · Some moments need more than words

  • Purpose: Introduce multimodal capture and record continuity.
  • Pictured headline/copy: “Some moments need more than words.” / “Write it, record it, photograph it or keep the files that matter.” Four actions: Text, Voice, Photo, Files. Do not request permissions merely by showing this screen. Current media capture foundations do not make this full product flow or its traceability claim complete; camera, voice, files, timestamps and provenance need separate implementation/acceptance.
  • Visual treatment: Light paper/sage interface with compact capture tiles, one combined sample record and a concise preservation note.

12 · A little clarity can change everything

  • Purpose: Introduce optional, evidence-linked understanding without diagnosis.
  • Pictured headline/copy: Headline “A little clarity can change everything.” The working title in the planning text was “Understand what you’re experiencing.” Supporting copy: “Explore possible patterns, understand your options and decide what happens next.” Example entry: “I keep having to ask permission before I can use my own money.” Possible topic: “Financial control”. The card says “This may reflect a controlling behaviour pattern. Understanding this can help you know what to ask, what to look out for or discuss with someone you trust.” Links/tiles: “Information and guidance”, “Ways to respond”, “Local support services”. Privacy note: “Identifying details are checked before anything is processed outside your device.”
  • Action and controls: Topic card/link and resource tiles; back and Continue.
  • Interaction/data: The illustration is not a live assessment, legal determination, diagnosis, or promise of safe external processing. Make any real interpretation opt-in, source-linked, uncertain, and grounded in reviewed resources. The pictured external-processing/privacy assurance is not established; do not show it until verified policy, technical behavior and evaluation support the exact claim. No raw record should be sent just because the screen offers interpretation.
  • Visual treatment: Dark editorial surface with a single selected-entry card, one possible-topic card and three compact information pathways; restrained gold highlight.

13 · You don’t have to do this alone

  • Purpose: Present support and connection as choices, including the choice to stay private.
  • Pictured headline/copy: “You don’t have to do this alone.” / “Share with someone you trust, explore available help or take your time.” Options: “Trusted companion — Share selected records, receive contributions or start a safety Trail”; “Professional support — Find counselling, legal assistance and verified support services”; “Keep it private — Continue documenting without sharing anything.” Reassurance: “You stay in control. You decide what to share, when to share it and who can see it.”
  • Action and controls: Three equal pathway cards; back and Continue.
  • Interaction/data: This is an introduction, not an invitation to add a contact, grant access, start location tracking or disclose records. Companion sharing, Trails, verified professional directory and referrals remain roadmap capabilities; do not imply that services or permissions are live. “Keep it private” must remain an equally valid choice.
  • Visual treatment: Warm ivory, friendly line icons, three balanced cards and muted reassurance panel; no pressure toward disclosure.

14 · Your space. Your control.

  • Purpose: Set up actual protected access and complete onboarding.
  • Pictured headline/copy: “Your space. Your control.” / “Protect your private space with a passkey. What you choose to share stays your decision.” Assurance cards: “Private access — Use your device’s passkey authentication to unlock LuLu’s private space”; “On-device by default — Private originals are stored on your device by default”; “Your permissions — External processing and sharing only happens with your permission.” Primary action: “Create my private space”.
  • Action and controls: Passkey/fingerprint illustration, three assurance cards and one enrolment action. No extra success/Continue gate is specified: a successful enrolment should enter the ordinary LuLu workspace directly.
  • Interaction/data: Unlike screen 8, this is real passkey enrolment. Handle cancellation, unsupported devices, existing accounts, network failure and retry without falsely completing onboarding or losing the first-entry draft. Each storage/processing assurance must match actual platform behavior and be reviewed; the current product has local encrypted private storage in some clients, but the browser-held key is not passkey-bound and there is no backup/recovery guarantee. Do not claim otherwise. A device-generic passkey prompt and wording is preferred over promising Face ID specifically.
  • Visual treatment: Dark warm charcoal surface, white wordmark and serif headline, large fingerprint symbol, three assurance cards and a bright high-contrast primary action. Keep room for OS UI and accessibility scaling.

Cross-screen behavior and states to settle

  • Decide whether Skip means skip the current screen, skip optional personalisation, or finish onboarding. The boards picture Skip repeatedly but the conversation does not define its semantics. Provide clear back, resume and safe exit behavior; do not make all 14 screens mandatory by implication.
  • Define how an existing signed-in user, an existing passkey/account, interrupted onboarding, passkey cancellation, storage failure, and a user returning to the pre-enrolment draft resume. Do not overwrite/delete existing records or duplicate the first entry on retry.
  • Define whether screen 3 is single or multi-select, whether screen 5 requires selection, and whether screen 6 autosaves before its explicit Save action. The images alone do not settle these contracts.
  • Ask for camera/microphone/file permissions only after an explicit use action and a short explanation. A denial must leave the user able to continue onboarding.
  • Support narrow and wide responsive layouts, safe areas, keyboard overlap, screen readers, visible focus, large text, contrast, reduced motion and non-color status cues. Keep controls reachable when the keyboard is open.
  • Preserve source originals and distinguish concealed, redacted, blurred, deleted and transformed states. Demo-only blur is not secure erasure or authorization.
  • Avoid legal evidence guarantees, diagnosis, guaranteed interpretation accuracy, guaranteed support availability, and broad privacy guarantees that have not been verified.

Current product boundary and implementation handoff

The app currently has a five-part welcome/orientation/create/sign-in experience in OnboardingAuth.tsx: welcome, two orientation screens, create passkey and returning sign-in. AuthBoundary.tsx restores native sessions where applicable and otherwise requires verified account authentication before the main app shell. Local email/password and Google alternatives now exist alongside passkeys, but their Firebase configuration and bridge release remain pending. Private content still requires passkey step-up. Successful authentication enters the app directly. This is a distinct existing flow, not the pictured 14-screen sequence; the existing sign-in-before-app direction also differs from screens 4–6 creating a first entry before screen 14 enrolment. Resolve that conflict and the pre-enrolment draft’s encryption, key ownership, recovery, cancellation and deletion behavior before implementation. Do not silently route it into the ordinary account journal or private vault.

Current code also includes private text draft capture/recovery, local media capture and storage, export/recovery-file crypto, and native platform adapters in files such as PrivateDraftComposer.tsx, mediaCapture.ts, localMediaVault.ts, privateExport.ts, recoveryFileCrypto.ts, and runtime.ts. These additions are newer than earlier design notes. Their presence does not establish that the 14-screen onboarding flow is integrated with them, that every pictured assurance is accurate, or that the complete capture/export/recovery lifecycle has been accepted on target devices. The existing passkey registration/sign-in, ordinary entry editor, private paragraph controls and quick exit are reuse candidates once the onboarding boundary and sequence are decided.
